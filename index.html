About Me

My name is Tom Rongen, and I am a PhD Candidate in Economics at Maastricht University. I am supervised by Frank Cörvers, Inge de Wolf, and Tom Stolp. My research is funded by Education Lab Netherlands and the Dutch Inspectorate of Education. My main focus is on teacher shortages in primary education. I exploit both existing administrative datasets and primary data to study how teacher shortages affect pupils and how teacher shortages might be mitigated. If you are interested in my research, feel free to contact me at tom.rongen@maastrichtuniversity.nl.

Ongoing Research (Thesis)

Predicting School-Level Teacher Shortages

Read Full Abstract

Many countries face persistent teacher shortages, which are considered as a threat to educational quality. This highlights the importance of examining the prevalence of teacher shortages and their implications for schools. However, shortages are often not measured directly at the school level, resulting in the use of various single proxies in the academic literature. Given the multifaceted nature of teacher shortages at the school-level, relying on a single indicator may provide only a partial and incomplete representation of the extent of shortages at schools. This study addresses this lack of standardisation by evaluating single-indicator proxies commonly used in the literature and developing a multi-indicator measure (proxy) for school-level teacher shortages. We identify teacher shortages using a a high-quality dataset covering 85 primary schools in the Netherlands, which includes an unique expert assessment of the teacher shortages at these schools (by the school principals and school inspectors). In addition, we constructed a comprehensive set of predictors drawing on both administrative and web-scraped data. We evaluate the performance of the proposed measures based on out-of-sample performance, parsimony, and validation analyses. Gradient Boosting outperforms other models in predicting school-level teacher shortages. Our results demonstrate that administrative and online-scraped data are able to explain 33% variance of school-level teacher shortages with an RMSE of 0.83, based on five times repeated 10-folds cross-validated estimates. Our multi-indicator proxy outperforms traditional single-indicator measures, with prediction accuracy increasing at more aggregated levels of prediction. The proxy provides a cost-effective approach to estimating and monitoring teacher shortages and can help signal emerging staffing challenges to school boards.

Teacher Shortages and educational (e)quality

Read Full Abstract

Educational systems worldwide have been faced with teacher shortages, raising concerns on the quality of education. While a substantial body of research has examined the effects of specific responses to shortages, such as larger class sizes, lower teacher quality, and reduced instructional time, less is known about the direct effects of teacher shortages on pupils. This study addresses this gap by examining how predicted school-level teacher shortages affect both the quality and equality of education. We use a previously validated proxy to measure school-level shortages and estimate their effects within a fixed effects framework. Educational quality is assessed using pupil-level standardized test scores, while educational equality is examined using multiple approaches, including interactions between shortages and prior achievement and a Gini coefficient based on the test score distribution. Our results indicate that teacher shortages are negatively associated with pupil achievement: a 10%pt increase in shortages is associated with a 0.003-0.004 SD decline in achievement. This relationship seems to be non-linear, with shortages appearing to have particularly detrimental effects on learning once they reach severe levels. In contrast we find no significant effects on educational equality. Our estimates remain robust after tracking and adjusting for omitted variable bias, attenuation bias, and aggregation bias.

The Effects of Improving Working Conditions on Teacher Turnover: Heterogeneous Responses by Work Stress Levels

Read Full Abstract

Teacher shortages remain a persistent challenge in Dutch primary education. Improving working conditions may help retain current teachers and attract former ones, but the relative effectiveness and cost-efficiency of specific measures are not well understood. This study uses a vignette experiment to estimate how changes in six key working conditions influence the turnover intentions of current and former primary school teachers in the Netherlands. We find that wage increases and the provision of teaching assistants have the largest effects on both retaining current teachers and attracting former teachers, though they are not the most cost-efficient. Increased control over working time emerges as the most cost-efficient intervention. Our results also show that teachers who report higher levels of work stress, measured through their need for recovery, are more likely to consider leaving the profession. At the same time, these teachers respond more strongly to enhanced working conditions than their less-stressed peers. This suggests that the impact of workplace improvements depends in part on teachers’ existing stress levels. Finally, heterogeneity analyses by baseline turnover intentions, sex, age, and experience are informative about potential compositional effects and sorting mechanisms of specific interventions. The findings underscore the value of targeted policies that address both structural working conditions and teacher well-being.
